US Trademark Law (Lanham Act) and the Absence of Sui Generis Geographical Indication Protection:

Unlike India, the United States does not have a dedicated, standalone Geographical Indication (GI) registration system comparable to India's Geographical Indications Act, 1999. This is one of the sharpest contrasts between the two jurisdictions relevant to Ayurveda IP strategy.

How the US protects geography-linked products instead: Rather than a separate GI registry, the US primarily protects geographic-origin-based products through its existing trademark framework under the Lanham Act, using two main mechanisms:

1. Certification marks: A certification mark indicates that goods or services meet certain standards (including geographic origin, material, mode of manufacture, quality, or other characteristics) set by the mark's owner, who does not use the mark on their own goods but certifies others who meet the standard. Certification marks can function similarly to GIs by certifying that a product genuinely originates from and is made according to the traditions of a specific region.

2. Collective marks: Used by members of a cooperative, association, or other collective group to indicate membership or to identify goods/services from that collective - this can also serve a function similar to GI protection for regional producer groups, though it operates through trademark registration rather than a dedicated GI system.

Practical implication for Ayurvedic products: A traditional Ayurvedic product tied to a specific Indian region seeking recognition/protection in the US market cannot rely on a GI registration transferring automatically - Indian GI status does not create automatic US legal protection. Instead, achieving similar protection in the US requires separately registering a certification mark or collective mark with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO), a different legal process with different requirements than India's GI Act.

Standard trademark protection: Beyond certification/collective marks, an individual company can also register a standard trademark for its specific Ayurvedic product brand name or logo, providing exclusive rights to use that specific mark in commerce - but this protects the brand identity, not the traditional knowledge or formulation itself, and does not prevent competitors from using the same traditional formulation under a different brand name.

Key takeaway for cross-jurisdiction strategy: An Ayurvedic product with GI-style regional recognition in India (e.g., a specific temple offering or region-specific formulation) does not carry that protection into the US market automatically. Companies seeking to protect the same geographic-origin association in both markets need to pursue two structurally different legal instruments: GI registration in India, and a certification or collective mark application with the USPTO for the US market.
