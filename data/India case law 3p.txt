Indian Case Law on Section 3(p) - Traditional Knowledge Exclusion:

Section 3(p) of the Patents Act, 1970 has been tested and interpreted by Indian courts in several significant cases, providing practical guidance on how the traditional knowledge exclusion actually applies.

Institute for Inner Studies v. Charlotte Anderson: In this case, the concept of "Pranic Healing," a facet of Yoga, was held to be part of India's traditional knowledge. The court ruled that Pranic Healing was barred from being patented on the grounds of public knowledge and lack of novelty, applying Section 3(p) directly. This case establishes that even modern-sounding wellness practices can be classified as traditional knowledge if they are rooted in established Yogic or Ayurvedic tradition, regardless of how the practice is branded or marketed.

Vandana Shiva v. Union of India: The Delhi High Court disallowed the patenting of micro-organisms in this case, on the grounds that the patent application was based on traditional knowledge of the life-form and its traditional use. The claimed invention lacked novelty because it was already in prior use. This case is significant because it extends the traditional knowledge exclusion beyond plant-based formulations to biological/microbial resources, showing that Section 3(p) is not limited to herbal remedies alone.

Maharshi Mahesh Jogi v. State of Madhya Pradesh: The Supreme Court in this case held that traditional knowledge gains legal legitimacy only if it can be tested and verified on the basis of objectivity, through demonstrable, verifiable evidence. This case is important because it clarifies that simply claiming something is "traditional knowledge" is not sufficient - courts require objective, testable evidence of the practice's traditional origin and use before according it TK status and the corresponding patent exclusion.

Practical significance for Ayurveda IP strategy: These cases collectively establish that Indian courts apply Section 3(p) broadly and require concrete evidence for both directions of the argument - a patent applicant cannot easily claim novelty over something with credible traditional roots, and equally, a challenger claiming "this is traditional knowledge" must be able to demonstrate that traditional use with objective evidence (similar to how CSIR used documented Sanskrit texts and published papers in the US turmeric case, rather than relying on undocumented oral tradition).

This connects directly to why the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) matters: it exists specifically to provide the kind of documented, verifiable evidence of traditional knowledge that the Maharshi Mahesh Jogi case says is legally necessary - transforming informal community knowledge into citable, court-admissible documentation.
