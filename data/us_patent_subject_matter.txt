U.S. Patent Eligibility (35 U.S.C. Section 101) and the Products-of-Nature Doctrine:

Section 101 of the U.S. Patent Act defines four categories of patent-eligible subject matter: any new and useful process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter. However, the U.S. Supreme Court has long held three judicial exceptions that fall outside these categories and cannot be patented: laws of nature, natural phenomena, and abstract ideas. For Ayurveda, the exception that matters most is natural phenomena, applied through what is called the "products of nature" doctrine.

Under the products-of-nature doctrine, a naturally occurring substance is not patent-eligible merely because it has been discovered or isolated from its natural surroundings. In Funk Brothers Seed Co. v. Kalo Inoculant Co. (1948), a mixture of naturally occurring bacteria was held unpatentable because it did not have any changed characteristics. In Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics (2013), isolated naturally occurring DNA was held to be a product of nature and not patent-eligible, while synthetic cDNA that does not occur in nature was eligible. The USPTO evaluates such claims using a "markedly different characteristics" analysis: compared with its natural counterpart, the claimed product must differ markedly in structure, function, or properties.

Applied to Ayurveda, a traditionally used herb (for example, Ashwagandha root) or a simple extract that has the same characteristics as the substance found in nature is generally NOT patent-eligible in the United States, because it is a product of nature. Eligibility becomes possible where human intervention creates something markedly different: a purified or modified compound with new functional properties, a novel formulation or composition of matter that behaves differently from its natural ingredients, a specific manufactured dosage form, or a method of treatment. Note that the U.S. allows claims to methods of medical treatment, which India does not. Any such claim must still separately satisfy novelty (Section 102), non-obviousness (Section 103), and enablement/written description (Section 112) — eligibility under Section 101 is only the first gate.

Contrast with India: India has no products-of-nature doctrine framed this way, but reaches overlapping results through Section 3(p) (traditional-knowledge exclusion), Section 3(e) (mere admixture / aggregation of known properties), and the bar on patenting methods of medical treatment. So a classical formulation is blocked in India mainly by Section 3(p), and a bare natural extract is blocked in the US mainly by the products-of-nature doctrine — different legal routes, similar outcome. This document pairs with us_patentability_criteria.txt, which covers the Section 102/103/112 requirements that apply after eligibility is established.

In short: in the US, nature as found is not patentable; what humans meaningfully change or invent from it may be, if it is also new, non-obvious, and fully described.
