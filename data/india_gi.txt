India's Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999:

A Geographical Indication (GI) is a sign used on products that have a specific geographical origin and possess qualities, reputation, or characteristics essentially attributable to that place of origin. Unlike a patent, a GI does not protect an invention - it protects the link between a product and its region of origin, and can apply indefinitely as long as the product continues to be made in that region using traditional methods.

Relevance to Ayurveda: Several Ayurvedic and herbal products have obtained GI registration in India, providing an alternative form of IP protection distinct from patents. Examples cited in legal literature include Palani Panchamirtham (a temple offering with claimed medicinal properties) and various region-specific herbal or Ayurvedic preparations tied to specific geographic areas of production.

Why GI matters for traditional knowledge protection: Since classical Ayurvedic formulations are generally barred from patent protection under Section 3(p) (as they constitute traditional knowledge), GI registration offers an indirect route to protect the commercial value of a region-specific Ayurvedic product. A GI does not prevent others from using the same traditional formulation, but it does prevent others from using the specific protected regional name/branding for products not actually originating from that certified region.

Registration process: A GI application is filed with the Geographical Indications Registry (located in Chennai) by an association of producers or a legally constituted body representing the producers of that good - individual producers typically cannot apply alone. The application must establish the geographical origin, the specific qualities linked to that origin, and the traditional production methods.

Distinction from patents: GI protection is collective (benefits all producers in the region using authentic methods) rather than exclusive to one applicant, and does not require novelty or inventive step - it requires proof of geographic-quality linkage and established reputation. This makes it fundamentally different from patent law's requirements and better suited to protecting traditional, place-based Ayurvedic knowledge that could never satisfy patent novelty requirements.

Limitation: GI protection does not prevent biopiracy in the same way patent-focused defenses (like TKDL) do - it protects branding/naming rights for a specific region's products, not the underlying formulation or traditional knowledge itself, which remains in the public domain and usable by anyone, just not under the protected geographical name.
