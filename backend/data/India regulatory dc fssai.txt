India's Regulatory Pathways for Ayurvedic Products - Drugs and Cosmetics Act & FSSAI:

Beyond IP protection, any Ayurvedic product intended for sale in India must also satisfy the correct regulatory pathway based on how it is classified and marketed. Choosing the wrong pathway is a common and costly mistake for AYUSH startups.

Pathway 1 - Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 (and Rules, 1945): Products marketed as Ayurvedic, Siddha, or Unani (ASU) medicines fall under this Act's specific provisions for ASU drugs. Manufacturing requires a license from the state licensing authority, and classical formulations (drawn from authoritative texts listed in the First Schedule of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act) can generally be manufactured under simpler licensing than "patent or proprietary" Ayurvedic medicines, which require additional documentation on formulation and safety since they are not directly drawn from classical texts.

Pathway 2 - FSSAI Ayurveda-Aahar / Nutraceutical Route: If a product is marketed as a food supplement, functional food, or nutraceutical rather than a medicine, it instead falls under the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), specifically under the Food Safety and Standards (Health Supplements, Nutraceuticals...) Regulations. FSSAI introduced a specific "Ayurveda Aahar" logo/category to allow certain traditional food-based Ayurvedic preparations to be marketed clearly as food products rather than drugs, provided they do not make disease-treatment claims.

Key distinction - drug claims vs food claims: The critical factor determining which pathway applies is the nature of the claims made about the product. A product claiming to "cure," "treat," or "prevent" a specific disease is treated as a drug under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, requiring drug licensing and adherence to ASU manufacturing standards. A product making only general wellness or nutritional claims (without disease-specific claims) can potentially qualify under the FSSAI food/nutraceutical route, which has different (often less stringent) compliance requirements.

Why this matters alongside IP strategy: A company's regulatory classification decision directly affects its patent strategy. A product regulated as a "new ASU drug" (non-classical, requiring safety and efficacy data) has a stronger case for patent protection under novelty/inventive step criteria than a classical formulation marketed as a food supplement, which is both harder to patent (Section 3(p) concerns) and subject to different, generally lighter-touch food safety regulation rather than drug efficacy requirements.

Practical guidance: Before evaluating whether an Ayurvedic product is patentable, it is necessary to first establish which regulatory category the product falls under (classical ASU medicine, new/patent ASU drug, or FSSAI nutraceutical/Ayurveda-Aahar product) since the answer significantly changes both the applicable regulatory compliance pathway and the realistic patent strategy.
