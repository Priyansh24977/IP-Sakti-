US FDA Botanical Drug Development Pathway:

If an Ayurvedic or herbal product is intended to be marketed in the US as a drug (making disease treatment/prevention claims) rather than a dietary supplement, it must go through the FDA's drug approval process. The FDA has a specific guidance pathway for "botanical drugs" that acknowledges the unique characteristics of plant-based products compared to single-molecule synthetic drugs.

IND (Investigational New Drug) application: Before conducting clinical trials on humans in the US, a company must file an IND application with the FDA, providing data on the botanical product's composition, manufacturing controls, and preclinical (animal/lab) safety data. The FDA's Botanical Drug Development guidance allows certain botanical products with a documented history of human use (including traditional use, such as in Ayurveda) to rely partly on that historical use as supporting safety evidence, potentially reducing some preclinical testing burden compared to a completely novel synthetic compound.

NDA (New Drug Application): Following successful clinical trials demonstrating safety and efficacy, a New Drug Application must be submitted and approved before the botanical drug can be marketed as a treatment for a specific disease or condition. This is a substantially longer, more expensive, and more rigorous pathway than the DSHEA dietary supplement route, but it allows the product to make specific disease-treatment claims that a supplement cannot legally make.

Unique challenges for botanical drugs: Unlike single-molecule drugs, botanical products often contain multiple active compounds working together, making it harder to isolate and characterize a single "active ingredient." The FDA's botanical guidance specifically addresses this by allowing well-characterized botanical mixtures (rather than single isolated compounds) to be approved as drugs, provided manufacturing consistency and quality controls can be demonstrated batch to batch.

Notable precedent: Veregen (sinecatechins, a green tea extract) and Fulyzaq/Crofelemer (derived from a South American tree) are examples of botanical drugs that have received FDA approval through this pathway, demonstrating that plant-based, traditionally-used botanical products can successfully navigate US drug approval, though the exact approval dates and current regulatory status should be verified if cited as specific precedent in a demo.

Relevance to Ayurveda IP strategy: A company choosing to pursue the FDA Botanical Drug Development pathway (rather than DSHEA) opens the door to stronger patent protection, since a drug with FDA-approved specific disease claims and demonstrated clinical efficacy data is more clearly "new" and "non-obvious" for patent purposes than a general-wellness dietary supplement. However, this comes with substantially higher costs, longer timelines, and more stringent Section 101/102/103 patent scrutiny given the higher visibility and higher stakes of an approved drug claim.
