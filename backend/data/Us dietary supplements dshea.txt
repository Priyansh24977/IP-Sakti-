United States Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA), 1994:

DSHEA is the primary US federal law governing how dietary supplements (including many Ayurvedic herbal products marketed in the US) are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). It creates a regulatory category distinct from both conventional food and prescription drugs.

No premarket approval requirement: Unlike drugs, dietary supplements do NOT require FDA approval before being marketed. Manufacturers are responsible for ensuring their products are safe and that label claims are truthful and not misleading, but they do not need to prove safety or efficacy to the FDA in advance, unlike the drug approval process. The FDA's role is primarily post-market - it can take action against unsafe products or false claims only after they reach the market.

Structure/function claims vs disease claims: Under DSHEA, a supplement can make "structure/function claims" (e.g., "supports immune health," "promotes healthy digestion") without FDA premarket approval, but these claims must be accompanied by a specific disclaimer stating the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. Making an actual disease claim (e.g., "treats arthritis") would reclassify the product as an unapproved drug, subjecting it to much stricter FDA drug regulations and enforcement.

New Dietary Ingredient (NDI) notification: If a supplement contains a "new dietary ingredient" - one not marketed in the US dietary supplement market before October 15, 1994 - the manufacturer must submit an NDI notification to the FDA at least 75 days before marketing, providing evidence of the ingredient's expected safety. Many Ayurvedic herbs and extracts, if not previously marketed in the US, may require this NDI notification process.

Current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP): Supplement manufacturers must comply with cGMP requirements specific to dietary supplements (21 CFR Part 111), covering manufacturing, packaging, labeling, and holding operations, to ensure product identity, purity, strength, and composition.

Relevance to Ayurveda IP and market entry strategy: DSHEA provides a comparatively faster and lower-cost market entry route for Ayurvedic products in the US compared to pursuing FDA drug approval, provided the product is marketed strictly as a supplement with structure/function claims rather than disease-treatment claims. This regulatory pathway choice should be made in conjunction with patent strategy, since a product marketed under DSHEA as a supplement (making only structure/function claims) is generally harder to justify as a "new drug" for patent purposes, compared to a product pursuing the FDA Botanical Drug Development pathway with disease-specific claims and clinical evidence.
