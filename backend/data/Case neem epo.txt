The Neem Patent Case (EPO Patent EP0436257) - Biopiracy and Prior Use:

Background: European Patent EP0436257, titled "Method for controlling fungi on plants by the aid of a hydrophobic extracted neem oil," was granted to the United States Department of Agriculture and the W.R. Grace Corporation. The patent's core claim covered a method of controlling fungi on plants using a Neem oil formulation containing hydrophobic extracted Neem oil (substantially free of azadirachtin), an emulsifying surfactant, and water.

The Challenge: In 1995, a coalition led by Dr. Vandana Shiva's Research Foundation for Science, Technology and Ecology, together with the International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements (IFOAM) and Belgian politician Magda Aelvoet, filed a formal legal opposition against the patent at the EPO. Their central argument was that Neem's fungicidal properties had been used in India for centuries and were part of the public domain, meaning the patent lacked novelty.

EPO Decision - First Ruling: On May 10, 2000, the EPO's Opposition Division revoked the patent in its entirety, finding that the claimed formulation lacked novelty and an inventive step when compared against a prior published paper documenting similar Neem oil fungicidal use. Note: unlike India's Section 3(p), the EPO does not have a direct "traditional knowledge exclusion" category - the revocation was reached through standard novelty and inventive-step analysis, using documented prior use as evidence, similar in mechanism to how the US turmeric case was decided under 35 U.S.C. Section 102/103.

Appeal and Final Outcome: The patent holders (by then the US and Thermo Trilogy, having acquired W.R. Grace's interest) appealed the decision. On March 8, 2005, the EPO's Technical Board of Appeal dismissed the appeal and upheld the revocation, bringing the ten-year legal battle to a close. This made the Neem case the first biopiracy-motivated patent challenge to be fully and finally concluded through the EPO's legal process.

Why this case matters alongside the turmeric case: The Neem and turmeric cases are often cited together as the two landmark examples of successfully challenging patents on traditional Indian knowledge - one at the EPO (Neem), one at the USPTO (turmeric). Both cases succeeded because documented, citable prior art was available to demonstrate lack of novelty; both cases directly contributed to the momentum behind creating the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL), so that similar documented evidence could be made available to patent examiners proactively, rather than requiring a decade-long legal battle after the fact.
