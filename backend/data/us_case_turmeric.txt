The Turmeric Patent Case (US Patent 5,401,504) — Traditional Knowledge as Prior Art:

The turmeric patent case is the best-known example of a US patent on traditional knowledge being overturned, and it is the case that led directly to India building the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library.

Background: Researchers Suman K. Das and Hari Har P. Cohly filed a patent application on December 28, 1993, which was granted as US Patent 5,401,504 on March 28, 1995, titled "Use of turmeric in wound healing," assigned to the University of Mississippi Medical Center. The claims covered a method of promoting wound healing by administering an effective amount of turmeric powder to a patient, either orally or topically.

CSIR's Challenge: India's Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) filed a request for re-examination at the USPTO on October 28, 1996, arguing the claimed use was not novel because it was already publicly known before the filing date. CSIR submitted 32 prior art references, including ancient Sanskrit texts and a 1953 paper in the Journal of the Indian Medical Association, documenting turmeric's long-standing traditional use for wound healing in India.

USPTO Decision: The USPTO's first office action, issued March 28, 1997, rejected all six of the patent's claims as anticipated and obvious in light of CSIR's submitted references, under 35 U.S.C. Sections 102 and 103. The University of Mississippi Medical Center did not contest this rejection and transferred its rights to the inventors, who briefly argued that turmeric powder and paste had different physical properties before the claims were ultimately cancelled later in 1997. (Note: published sources give slightly different dates for the final revocation order, ranging from August to November 1997 - confirm the exact date against USPTO file history before citing a specific date in a live demo.)

Why the case matters legally. Under 35 U.S.C. Section 102, an invention cannot be patented if it was already known or described in a printed publication before the filing date. Traditional knowledge is fully capable of destroying novelty in the United States - but only if it is documented in a form an examiner can find and cite. CSIR won not because the knowledge was ancient, but because it could produce 32 specific, citable written references. Undocumented oral tradition, however widely practised, is far harder to use as prior art because examiners cannot search it.

Why the case matters practically. This is the direct origin of the TKDL. India's response was to systematically document traditional Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha and Yoga knowledge from classical texts, translate it into languages examiners actually read, and map it to patent classification codes so it surfaces in ordinary prior-art searches. Instead of fighting wrongful patents one by one after grant, at great cost, the TKDL prevents them from being granted in the first place. See tkdl_overview.txt for how that access works in practice.

The lesson for applicants. If your invention is essentially a traditional use of a traditional material, it is vulnerable in the United States on novelty grounds under Section 102, and separately vulnerable on eligibility grounds under Section 101 as a product of nature (see us_patent_subject_matter.txt). A defensible US application generally needs something the tradition does not already disclose - a specific purified compound, a genuinely novel formulation, a new delivery mechanism, or a demonstrated unexpected effect - and it needs to be drafted knowing that TKDL will be searched.

Related cases follow the same pattern: the neem patent granted by the European Patent Office was revoked after opposition, and the RiceTec basmati patent in the US was substantially narrowed after challenge. In each case, documented prior art was the deciding factor.
